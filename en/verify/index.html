Deutsche FassungLast checked: 6 September 2026

Every recommendation on this site rests on operator statements and on measurements. Statements can be quoted; measurements can be repeated. This section shows how.

All commands were run on 7 September 2026 on an ordinary Linux machine, and the outputs described are what actually came back. Where output can differ, it says so. You need nothing but dig, curl and openssl; all three are present on most systems. On Windows, the Windows Subsystem for Linux provides the same tools.

One note up front, because it is the most common source of error: always query the resolver directly, using @ and its address. Without @ you get an answer from your operating system's local service, and that answer says little about the resolver itself.

Does my resolver validate DNSSEC?

Three commands answer this question, and they answer it beyond doubt. What matters is querying the resolver directly, using @ and its address. Without @ you are asking the operating system's local stub, which often swallows the decisive detail.

The positive control

Does the resolver mark a correctly signed answer as verified?

dig +dnssec @9.9.9.9 sigok.ippacket.stream A

The line ;; flags: must contain ad alongside qr rd ra, and the status must be NOERROR. Measured on 7 September 2026: status: NOERROR and flags: qr rd ra ad. The ad stands for “authentic data”. RFC 4035 permits a server to set this bit only if it has verified every record in the answer itself.

Replace 9.9.9.9 with the address you want to test.

The negative control

Does the resolver reject a deliberately mis-signed answer?

dig @9.9.9.9 sigfail.ippacket.stream A
dig @9.9.9.9 dnssec-failed.org A

Both must return status: SERVFAIL and an empty answer section. Measured on 7 September 2026: both SERVFAIL. If NOERROR comes back with an IP address instead, this resolver does not validate. Two independent test domains, because either one can go away at any time.

Proof that DNSSEC was really the reason

A SERVFAIL can have many causes. How do I rule the others out?

dig +cd @9.9.9.9 sigfail.ippacket.stream A

With the CD bit set, meaning “checking disabled”, the same query must now return NOERROR and an address. Measured on 7 September 2026: without +cd SERVFAIL, with +cd NOERROR. That switch is the proof: the resolver checked first and refused for that reason, not because the domain was broken or the server overloaded.

Reading the reason in plain text

Does the resolver also say why it refused?

dig @9.9.9.9 sigfail.ippacket.stream A | grep EDE

A line of the form ; EDE: 6 (DNSSEC Bogus). That is exactly what was measured on 7 September 2026. Extended DNS Errors per RFC 8914 give a machine-readable reason instead of a bare SERVFAIL. If no EDE line appears, the resolver does not support the extension; on its own that says nothing about its validation.

The pitfall that ruins most tests

Why is the ad flag missing even though the resolver validates?

dig +dnssec sigok.ippacket.stream A
dig +dnssec @9.9.9.9 sigok.ippacket.stream A

The difference is the @. Without an address you are asking the operating system's local service. On the test machine the first command returned flags: qr rd ra without ad on 7 September 2026, the second flags: qr rd ra ad. So the upstream does validate, the local stub simply does not pass the marking through. Anyone unaware of this will mistake a validating resolver for a broken one.

On Linux, resolvectl status shows what the system service is doing.

Which resolver am I actually using?

What is configured and what is used are two different things. Routers, VPN software, browsers with their own DoH and the system service all interfere. These commands show who really answers in the end, and which part of your address they pass on.

Who answers, and what do they pass on about me?

Does the authoritative server see my address, or only the resolver's?

dig +short TXT whoami.ds.akahelp.net

This Akamai service answers with up to three pairs. ns is the address of the resolver that actually asked. If ecs appears as well, part of your own address was sent along. Measured on 7 September 2026 through the system resolver: only "ns", no ecs. So nothing of the client address was passed on.

One provider, two addresses, two outcomes

What does the difference between with and without EDNS Client Subnet actually look like?

dig +short TXT whoami.ds.akahelp.net @9.9.9.9
dig +short TXT whoami.ds.akahelp.net @9.9.9.11

Measured on 7 September 2026. Via 9.9.9.9 only "ns" came back with the resolver address. Via 9.9.9.11 there were additionally "ip" and "ecs" "203.0.113.0/24/24", meaning your own network with a truncated address. Same operator, same privacy policy, one character of difference in the address. This is the cleanest demonstration of ECS, because every other condition stays the same.

The network shown is an example. Your own output will contain your own network.

Which node of an anycast network answers

Anycast means many servers, one address. Which one is it right now?

dig +nsid @9.9.9.9 example.com A

In the OPT PSEUDOSECTION a line appears reading ; NSID: ... ("res701.qfra3"). That is what was measured on 7 September 2026; the identifier points to a location in Frankfurt. If the line is missing, the server sends no identifier. The advantage over the older query dig @server CH TXT id.server is that the identifier travels along with the normal answer.

Is the answer really encrypted?

An encrypted connection on its own means nothing as long as nobody checks who is at the other end. That is the most common mistake in guides: they show a successful handshake and call it security.

DNS over TLS in one command

Does the answer really arrive through the TLS channel?

dig +tls @dns.quad9.net example.com A

The decisive line is the penultimate one. Measured on 7 September 2026: ;; SERVER: 149.112.112.112#853(dns.quad9.net) (TLS). The (TLS) and port 853 are the evidence. If (TLS) is absent, the query ran in the clear.

The most common mistake: encrypted but unverified

Encryption without certificate checking protects against whom exactly?

echo QUIT | openssl s_client -connect dns.quad9.net:853 \
  -servername dns.quad9.net -alpn dot 2>&1 \
  | grep -E 'subject=|Verification|ALPN protocol'

Three lines must come together. Measured on 7 September 2026: subject=C=CH, ST=Zurich, L=Zürich, O=Quad9, CN=dns.quad9.net, then Verification: OK, then ALPN protocol: dot. Only all three together establish encrypted, correct counterpart, correct protocol. A handshake alone establishes only that somebody answered.

With kdig from the package knot-dnsutils (Debian, Ubuntu) or knot-utils (Fedora, RHEL) the same thing is shorter: kdig -d @dns.quad9.net +tls-ca +tls-host=dns.quad9.net example.com A. Note that +tls on its own does not verify the certificate; that requires +tls-ca.

The counter-test that has to fail

Does my tool really check, or does it wave everything through?

echo QUIT | openssl s_client -connect 9.9.9.9:853 \
  -servername dns.quad9.net -alpn dot -verify_return_error \
  -verify_hostname wrong.example.com 2>&1 | grep -iE 'verif|error' | head -3

This command has to fail. Expected is a line reporting a verification error about the hostname. If a successful verification comes back here, all your other checks are worthless. A negative control that does not fail measures nothing.

Checking DNS over QUIC

Does the endpoint really speak DoQ, or only almost?

openssl s_client -quic -alpn doq -connect dns.quad9.net:853 \
  -servername dns.quad9.net < /dev/null 2>&1 \
  | grep -E 'ALPN protocol|Verification'

Measured on 7 September 2026: Verification: OK and ALPN protocol: doq. A word on interpretation: a negotiated doq proves only that the server accepts the protocol. Whether an answer follows is a separate question. That very difference showed up at UncensoredDNS, where the handshake succeeded but a real query went unanswered.

Requires OpenSSL 3.5 or newer. Check with openssl version.

DNS over HTTPS, the convenient way and the exact way

Does the DoH endpoint answer the way RFC 8484 requires?

dig +https @dns.quad9.net example.com A

curl -sS -o antwort.bin -w '%{http_code} %{content_type} %{size_download}\n' \
  -H 'accept: application/dns-message' \
  'https://dns.quad9.net/dns-query?dns=AAABAAABAAAAAAAAA3d3dwdleGFtcGxlA2NvbQAAAQAB'

The first command is the convenient one: dig speaks DoH itself and shows a readable answer. The second shows what actually goes over the wire. Measured on 7 September 2026: 200 application/dns-message 65. The file contains a binary DNS message, not JSON. The first bytes read 00 00 81 80 00 01 00 02: identifier zero as RFC 8484 requires, response bit set, one question, two answers.

The base64 string in the example appears verbatim in RFC 8484 itself and asks for www.example.com. Some providers additionally offer a JSON interface; it is convenient, but it is not a standard and does not behave the same everywhere.

What else does my query reveal?

Two properties decide how much a resolver gives away about you, and how much an observer can infer from packet size alone. Each can be measured with a single command.

QNAME minimisation

Does every server along the way learn the full name, or only as much as it needs?

dig +short TXT qnamemintest.internet.nl @9.9.9.9

The answer contains exactly one of two sentences. Measured on 7 September 2026: "HOORAY - QNAME minimisation is enabled on your resolver :)!". The opposite reads "NO - QNAME minimisation is NOT enabled on your resolver :(". Alongside it you always get the line a.b.qnamemin-test.internet.nl. and an SPF record; the order varies.

The test service is operated by SIDN Labs and NLnet Labs.

Does the resolver pad its answers?

Encryption hides the content. Does it hide the length as well?

dig +tls +padding=468 A example.com @9.9.9.9
dig +tls +padding=468 A example.com @1.1.1.1

A difference worth seeing. Measured on 7 September 2026: at 9.9.9.9 the PAD line was absent and ;; MSG SIZE rcvd: 72 showed the unpadded size. At 1.1.1.1 there appeared ; PAD: (392 bytes) and ;; MSG SIZE rcvd: 468. Cloudflare padded the answer to a uniform length, Quad9 did not on that day.

Why this matters: RFC 9076 points out that inferences can be drawn from the size and timing patterns of encrypted messages. Padding per RFC 7830 removes the basis for that analysis. A single measurement on one day is not, however, a statement about continuous operation.

Why the test domains have no website

A note on the test domains: sigok.ippacket.stream, sigfail.ippacket.stream, dnssec-failed.org and qnamemintest.internet.nl have no website you could visit. They exist only in the DNS. With dnssec-failed.org that is even necessary: if your resolver is doing its job it cannot resolve the name at all, so no page can open. Here the absence of the page is the proof, not the fault.
